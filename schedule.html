How the two modes differ

Twitch's API doesn't expose a stream's real timezone, only its broadcast language. "Time of day" estimates each stream's local clock from a language→region lookup (documented in EDA.py as LANGUAGE_TZ_OFFSET_HOURS) — a deliberate approximation, most useful for spotting whether local evenings simply beat local mornings everywhere. "Standardized" needs no such guess: it's the literal UTC clock, and is what you'd use to target a specific real-world audience window.

Exactly how is each cell's color calculated?

Every cell starts as a single number: total viewers \u00f7 unique streamers for that day+hour slot (using whichever timezone mode is selected above, and only the region you've filtered to, if any). That's viewers-per-streamer, not raw viewer volume — a slot with huge total viewership but also huge streamer competition won't look artificially "hot".

That raw ratio is then turned into a color level 0–5 relative to the single best cell in the current view: level = min(5, ceil((ratio / max_ratio) × 5)). So a cell's brightness always reflects how close it is to the best slot for the filters you've picked, not a fixed number — switching timezone or region can reshuffle which cells look brightest.

Level 0–1 — quiet, low ratio Level 2–3 — moderate Level 4–5 — wide open, best window

All six levels are the same mint-green hue at increasing opacity/glow — it's an intensity scale, not a red-to-green scale. The single outlined cell is "best overall": the intersection of the independently-highest day and independently-highest hour, which isn't always literally the single brightest cell in the grid.

Ratios are total viewers divided by distinct concurrent streamers for that day/hour bucket, aggregated across all months in the dataset. Empty cells mean the scraper simply never took a snapshot in that slot.
